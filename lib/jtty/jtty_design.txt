JTTY: A possible WSJT mode for fast RTTY-like contest exchanges

Our general goal is an operational feel similar to standard ham RTTY,
but with much better weak-signal performance and much smaller error
rate. JTTY transmissions can start at any time and typically last a
few seconds. Any arbitrary message can be sent (up to a 70-character
line length), with upper-case letters, digits, spaces, and
punctuation. Source encoding is especially well optimized for short,
fixed-format messages that are frequently used in RTTY-style
contesting.

A JTTY transmission is composed of one or more frames, each with a
synchronising sequence followed by a data block with 32-bit
information payload. Two payload bits select one of 4 possible message
types; the remainder convey details of user information for that
type. The payload is large enough to carry a standard callsign plus
flags to convey common message words like "CQ", "TU", "599", "AGN?",
and "TU NOW". A frame can also convey a sequence of any five
characters chosen from the supported 64-character ASCII subset.

To ensure decoded message integrity, a 10-bit CRC is appended to each
32-bit payload. The result is then encoded using a strong binary block
code with parameters (n,k) = (80,42). The code is a shortened form of
the (128,90) LDPC code used for MSK144 (Franke and Taylor, QEX,
July/August 2017).

JTTY uses Gaussian-smoothed 4-tone frequency-shift keying (4-GFSK).
Each frame has a 13-symbol synchronization sequence followed by 40
symbols carrying the message payload and error-control bits.  At the
currently selected symbol rate, 12000/384 = 31.25 baud, a JTTY frame
is transmitted in 1.696 seconds. Nominal signal bandwidth is 125 Hz,
and 99% of transmitted power falls within bandwidth xxx Hz.

Source encoding is optimized for message formats typically used in
RTTY-style contesting. Repetition of important message fragments such
as callsigns and contest serial numbers is generally not needed, since
JTTY's strong FEC already implies reliable copy. Most contest-style
transmissions require only one or two frames, and thus have duration
1.7 or 3.4 s. Received message portions are displayed to the operator
sequentially, on the fly, rather than after the end of a
transmission. There is no need for archaic RTTY necessities such as
CR/LF at start of a transmission, LETTERS/NUMBERS shift characters, an
extra space at end-of-message, etc. Achievable QSO rates for JTTY in
contesting circumstances will be significantly higher than for
standard 45-baud RTTY. JTTY will be reliable at signal levels well
below those needed for RTTY, and there will be far less on-screen
garbage.

An example showing the messages exchanged for two successive
contest-style QSOs is shown below, where I have also listed the number
of frames required for each message. In this sequence KA1ABC calls CQ,
gets decoded replies from WB9XYZ and JA6DEF, and works them both in
sequence -- including a requested repeat from JA6DEF.

---------------------------------------------
Run station             S+P stations   Frames
---------------------------------------------
CQ KA1ABC CQ                             1
                        WB9XYZ           1
                        JA6DEF           1
WB9XYZ 599 101                           2
                        599 057          1
TU NOW JA6DEF 599 102                    2
                        (no decode)      1
JA6DEF AGN?                              1
                        599 292          1
TU KA1ABC CQ                             1
---------------------------------------------

These two example contest QSOs use a total of 9 transmission
intervals and 12 JTTY frames. The average transmission length is thus
about 1.7*12/9 = 2.3 s. With another ~0.7 s for T/R switching,
hardware and software latencies, and operator reaction time, we get
something like 3 s per T/R interval and perhaps 15 s per QSO. If
sustained, this rate would yield 240 QSOs/hour.

Source-encoding of JTTY messages involves pack() and unpack() routines
similar to those used in other WSJT-X modes.
